Why Fair Water Pricing Can Make India Water Positive

India's water is priced below cost across agriculture, industry, and municipalities. Fair pricing isn't about making water expensive — it's about making its true value visible.

Sunil Pachar

Founder, Ecolive Ventures

India's water pricing problem is structural. Across agriculture—which accounts for 89% of freshwater withdrawals—water is effectively free. Industrial users pay tariffs that cover, at best, operation and maintenance costs. Municipal water, where it reaches households at all, is priced at a fraction of its true cost. The result is predictable: overuse, underinvestment, and a crisis that accelerates with every year of GDP growth.

What "fair" pricing means

Fair water pricing is not about making water unaffordable for the poor. It means pricing water for industrial and high-volume commercial users at its full economic cost—including the cost of replenishment, treatment, and the externalities of contamination. It means removing the implicit subsidy that flows to water-intensive industries while the aquifers beneath them decline.

The conservation case

Every study on industrial water conservation finds the same thing: the most powerful driver of efficiency investment is price. When water costs reflect its true value, the business case for rainwater harvesting, effluent recycling, and process optimisation writes itself. Subsidised water is subsidised waste.

The policy pathway

Progressive water pricing—where the first tranche of consumption is affordable and subsequent tranches carry the full economic cost—has worked in Israel, Singapore, and parts of Australia. India has the institutional framework to implement it. What is missing is political will and industry coalition pressure.

What companies can do now

Companies don't need to wait for policy. Internal water pricing—treating water as a line item with a real cost in operations budgets—produces the same conservation effect at the facility level. Start there.
