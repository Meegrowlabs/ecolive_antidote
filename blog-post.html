Water Stewardship 14 min read 02 April 2026

How 652 million litres came back to the ground — without a single new dam.

A four-year retrospective on the WATER framework: the recharge structures, the village-level data we collect, and the corporate CSR partners that financed them.

Sunil Pachar

Founder, Ecolive Ventures

Four years ago, when we set out to write down what we'd learned about groundwater recharge, the working title was "What we got wrong." It still describes most of the early chapters. But the longer we worked, the more it became clear that the system itself — what we now call WATER — wasn't a list of mistakes; it was a framework for thinking about water as a closed loop, not a one-way pipe.

This is a long-form retrospective. If you are an ESG officer, a CSR partner, or a city engineer evaluating a recharge project, the section on how we measure recharge is probably the one to read first. If you are an investor, the unit economics section is the one that will surprise you.

The WATER framework, in a paragraph

WATER is an acronym we settled on after years of failing to explain what we did at conferences. It stands for Water avoidance through efficiency, Availability through reuse, Augmentation through recycling, and Enhanced recharge. The order matters. Reduction comes before recycling, which comes before recharge — and recharge, the part everyone wants to talk about, is the last thing you should be doing if the first three are still bleeding water.

"Water isn't scarce. The knowledge to hold it is."

Measuring recharge without measuring recharge

Direct measurement of groundwater recharge is, charitably, a hard problem. Most CSR audits get around this by counting structures built — number of pits, length of bunds, area of catchment. We count those too, but they are inputs. The number that actually matters is the change in the local aquifer, and that requires three things you cannot fake: pre-project well levels, post-monsoon well levels, and a control site within 5 km that did not receive intervention.

What our village reports look like

  • Six monitoring wells per project, surveyed quarterly
  • Electrical conductivity samples to detect dilution from rainfall vs. recycled water
  • Photographs of catchments and structures with GPS-stamped metadata
  • A signed beneficiary log — the families whose wells the project is meant to serve

None of this is novel. What is unusual is that we publish all of it, by project, on a quarterly cadence. You can ask any of our CSR partners for the raw spreadsheets and they will send them. This is not a marketing decision; it is the only way we have found to make CSR money behave like grant money instead of like advertising spend.

The unit economics, four years in

Across 1,150 completed projects, the average cost per litre of verified annual recharge sits at ₹0.07. The headline number — 652 million litres — translates to roughly ₹4.5 crore of cumulative CSR spend, distributed across 11 states. Per-project costs vary wildly, from ₹40,000 for a single farm pond to ₹18 lakh for a multi-village watershed cluster, but the per-litre figure is remarkably stable across geographies.

The interesting question, when we sat down with the data, was not "how do we lower the cost per litre?" but "why does the cost per litre stay flat as scale increases?" The answer, we think, has to do with what we call the community-ownership coefficient — and that is the subject of a separate piece, which we'll publish next month.

What's next
